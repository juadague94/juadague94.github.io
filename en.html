The method

Assessment, anatomy, adjustment, and practice.

I have refined this process over years, first on myself and then with hundreds of students. It adapts to the person but follows a consistent order: observe what you do, understand why it happens, adjust, and test again.

Understand what is breaking down. Build EQ you can repeat.

Equalization is not solved by accumulating attempts or copying a maneuver. We identify what changes in your coordination, train a sequence you can recognize, and bring it into the water with a clear purpose.

  • An individual technical review of your equalization pattern
  • Progressive exercises to build control and coordination
  • Dry practice between sessions, with guidance for transfer to the water

You do not choose by depth alone

Basic, Intermediate, and Advanced organize common entry points. They do not replace looking at your case: a simple problem can appear at depth, and an advanced skill may be needed earlier.

From the movement to the dive

We first isolate structures and coordination on dry land. Then we add complexity and use the water to check a skill that has been trained, not to discover it under pressure.

Your own sequence

I do not try to make everyone equalize in the same way. We look for a way of using your structures that you can understand, repeat, and adjust as context changes.

How we work on EQ

  1. 01 · Observe

    What is happening now

    We review tongue, glottis, larynx, and soft palate behavior; what changes upside down; and when tension, swallowing, or loss of control appears.

  2. 02 · Build

    A trainable sequence

    We turn observation into concrete work: simple dissociations, progressive coordination, and movements that come closer to what you will need at depth.

  3. 03 · Transfer

    From dry land to water

    You leave each session with work for dry training, pool practice, or your next open-water session. We review the response and adjust so the skill becomes more stable.

Common paths, not self-diagnoses

Prices and session counts are published so you have clarity. Our initial conversation helps choose a path based on what shows up in your equalization, not only on your depth.

Questions worth answering before you start

Do I need to know which level I need?

No. The levels are references for price and scope, not proof that they are your path. Tell me what happens, and we will review your starting point before beginning.

I do not know which path I need →
Can EQ be worked on online?

Yes. The technical work is built mainly on dry land, with time to observe, repeat, and correct without the urgency of a descent. Water helps check and transfer a skill you have already trained.

I want to understand the online process →
What happens between sessions?

After each session, I give you concrete dry work and, when appropriate, pool or open-water work. The goal is to build useful repetitions and arrive at the next conversation with information to adjust.

I want to understand the work between sessions →
Is this diagnosis medical?

No. It is a technical review of your equalization and coordination to guide training. If something needs clinical assessment, this course does not replace it.

I want to talk about my case →
Does Advanced EQ guarantee a depth?

No. The aim is to build clearer, more repeatable technique. Depth depends on more factors, and no course guarantees a performance outcome or a safe situation in the water.

I want to discuss my goals →

Tell me where the problem appears, what you have tried, and what you would like to do differently. From there, we can decide whether this process fits you.

San Andrés · Deeper by Nature.
Nirvana · San Andrés

I do not remember the exact date. I remember where it started.

First there was the water. Much later came medicine, psychotherapy, emergency care, Providencia after Hurricane IOTA, and competition from both sides of the line. Over time, they stopped feeling like separate stories.

01
Nirvana, before all of this

That is where I learned to go down.

I do not know whether I was nine, ten or eleven. It feels more honest to say that than to invent a date. What I do remember is Nirvana: the sea in San Andrés, the reef, going down to look and coming back to the surface. That was freediving. I was not thinking about hypoxia, equalization, efficiency or numbers; it was simply a very natural way of being in the water.

That place became tied to freediving long before I knew how much space the sport would later take in my life. By the time medicine arrived, I had already spent years learning about the body in a much less academic way.

02
Much later

Medicine gave names to old sensations.

At medical school I began to recognize in words things I had felt many times: pressure, gases, perfusion, metabolism, autonomic responses. Psychotherapy later added another layer: attention, anxiety, interpretation and behavior. It did not feel like discovering another world; it felt like learning another language for the same body.

That did not make freediving simpler. It made it more interesting, because knowing that something happened was no longer enough: I wanted to understand why it happened and when that knowledge was actually useful in the water.

03
Emergency medicine, disaster response and Providencia

Some decisions do not wait until you have finished thinking.

Before bringing medicine back into freediving, I worked in emergency care and in a disaster setting in Providencia after Hurricane IOTA. They are different environments, but they share something important: reality rarely arrives in order. Information is incomplete, people are frightened, resources are not always what you would want, and decisions still have to be made.

There I learned a part of medicine that does not fit neatly into textbooks. Assess quickly without becoming impulsive. Prioritize. Communicate clearly. Prepare for the worst scenario without behaving as if it were inevitable. And understand that a good protocol is not good because it looks good on paper, but because someone can use it when the situation becomes complicated.

That experience changed my relationship with risk. It did not make me conservative by default; it made me less tolerant of risk that adds nothing. There are situations where exposure makes sense and others where we are simply paying for poor preparation, overconfidence or bad organization. That distinction followed me back into the water.

04
Freediving from the medical side

Physician for the Colombian Cup in its last two editions.

I already knew competition from the athlete’s side: the waiting, the activation before an attempt, the feeling that months of work are compressed into a few minutes, and the instant when you know whether the dive is still organized or beginning to slip away. Later being there as a physician forced me to look at the same scenes with a different responsibility.

It was no longer about my attempt. I had to watch recoveries, recognize when something moved outside the expected range, anticipate what might be needed and think through the response before it was required. That role interested me because it brings together several things I had worked on separately for years: physiology, behavior, decision-making, team organization and the ability to act calmly when the margin becomes small.

Since then I have become increasingly interested in developing safer freediving practices. Not because freediving can be made risk-free—it cannot—and not because safety should become an excuse to train with fear. I am interested in something more concrete: separating the risk that belongs to depth from the risk we add through poor progression, weak protocols, unclear communication, rescues that work only in theory, or because nobody clearly defined what to do when the plan changes.

For me, safety is part of performance. An athlete can demand more from themselves when the system around them works; an instructor can teach better when they know what they are observing; a team can respond better when important decisions are not improvised. I am interested in building that kind of practice: serious enough to respect what can happen, and good enough not to take away what makes freediving fascinating.

05
The way back

And all of that came back into the water with me.

When I returned to training with more intention, the question stopped being only how deep I could go. I began looking at why a dive felt easy one day and difficult the next, how much was technical, how much physiological, what was happening with attention, and how much of the problem had already been built before entering the water.

Much of what I do today came from there: training, teaching equalization, reading papers, reviewing data, working with attention, thinking through protocols and continuing to test ideas at depth. I do not use medicine to remove the mystery from freediving. I use it to look more carefully, recognize what I still do not understand and, when possible, make exploration a little smarter and a little less dependent on luck.

Mental health · Freediving

Mental health care grounded in the context of freediving.

Sometimes what happens in the water is no longer only a performance problem: persistent anxiety, fear after an incident, pressure around a target, or a difficult relationship with body sensations. Freediving provides the context for this process, but the goal is mental health.

Different from coaching

Coaching integrates physical preparation, technique and mental skills to improve performance. Here we begin with a clinical assessment and a concrete formulation of the problem, even when it appears during training or competition.

Cognitive behavioral model

I work with cognition, behavior, avoidance, emotional regulation and behavioral experiments when they help change what is maintaining the problem.

Mindfulness with a clear purpose

It can help someone observe experience more clearly and change their relationship with sensations. I do not use it as an automatic answer to everything.
